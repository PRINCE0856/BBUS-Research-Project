## A. The strongest designs, ranked for transferability to BBUS

These are the five templates worth copying. Everything else in this file is
supporting evidence.

| Rank | Study | Design to copy | Why it fits BBUS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Halder & Jayadev (2025), Azim Premji University, Shakti scheme, BMTC Bengaluru | Route-level administrative trip records, 2.7-2.9 crore trips, Jan 2023 to Mar 2025 | Uses operator data, not survey. Cheapest possible design. Bengaluru is already a Group A city. Reported women's daily ridership up over 150%, settling near a 60:40 women:men split |
| 2 | Dasgupta & Datta (2023), Ashoka DP 105, Delhi Pink Pass | Difference-in-differences on a scheme start date, using Time Use Survey | Exactly the Group B logic. Reported large employment and paid-work-time effects for marginalised women. Verify the 24 percentage point figure, it is very large |
| 3 | Rathore & Singh (2026), arXiv 2604.14758 | Triple difference plus event study on staggered rollout across five states | The staggered PM e-Bus Sewa rollout has the same structure. Triple difference is stronger than plain DiD when city-level shocks are a worry |
| 4 | Martinez et al. (2020), IZA DP 12020, Lima Metropolitano | Causal DiD on staggered BRT and light rail opening, effects estimated separately by sex | The reference international study for transit and women's employment. Found gains for women only, not men, mostly on the extensive margin |
| 5 | Borker (2021), World Bank PRWP 9731, Delhi | Route-level safety scoring from crowdsourced audits, fed into a choice model | Converts safety from a soft perception item into a route attribute that changes choices. Reported women accept a much worse college for a safer route |

## B. India: fare-free bus schemes for women

The single richest natural experiment available to BBUS. Multiple states, staggered.

| Study | Where | Design | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dasgupta & Datta 2023, Ashoka DP 105 | Delhi | DiD, Time Use in India Survey 2019 | Employment of marginalised women up 24pp; about 150 min/day more paid work. VERIFY, effect is implausibly large at face value |
| Rathore & Singh 2026, arXiv 2604.14758 | 5 states | Triple difference, event study, two Time Use Survey rounds | Effects concentrated in early-adopter states; larger where mobility restrictions on women are tighter |
| Halder & Jayadev 2025, Azim Premji University | Karnataka, BMTC | Administrative route-level trip records | Women's ridership up over 150%; stabilised near 60:40 |
| JustJobs Network 2025, Shakti Yojane | Karnataka | Two-phase panel survey plus interviews and focus groups | About 19% of lower-income women reported a new or better-paying job; over 80% saved up to Rs 500/week |
| Jamba, Devaraj & Kanuri 2025, WRI India | Delhi | Ridership records plus household survey, descriptive | Women save up to 8% of monthly household income; ridership up about 20%, 2019-2023 |
| CAG 2023 | Tamil Nadu, 7 cities | Interviews, n=3,000 | 2,659 of 3,000 said travel patterns changed |
| Ratra & Kumar 2026, Research in Transportation Economics | Delhi | Time Use Survey comparison | Inclusivity of gendered subsidy. Effect sizes not confirmed |

Seven-state comparative synthesis also exists (2026, ResearchGate). It reports
the consistent pattern plus the downsides: overcrowding, fiscal strain, and
exclusion of groups the scheme does not cover. Useful for balance.

## C. International: transit and women's economic participation

| Study | Where | Design | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Martinez et al. 2020, IZA DP 12020 / IDB | Lima | Staggered BRT and rail expansion, causal | Employment and hourly earnings up for women only. Extensive margin |
| Garlick, Field, Vyborny & Subramanian 2024/25, IZA DP 17883 | Urban Pakistan | RANDOMISED CONTROLLED TRIAL, women-only vs mixed transport at varying prices | Women-only transport more than doubled job application rates. The only true RCT in this scan |
| Tsivanidis 2026, American Economic Review 116(2) | Bogota TransMilenio | Quantitative spatial general equilibrium plus reduced-form elasticities | Large aggregate welfare gains, roughly proportionate across skill levels |
| World Bank 2016, TRB paper | Bogota | DiD on a pro-poor targeted fare subsidy | Income rose for residents near, but not immediately adjacent to, trunk stations |
| Alam, Bagnoli & Kerzhner 2023, World Bank PRWP 10404 | Amman, Beirut, Cairo | Cross-city regression, 2022 mobility surveys plus built-environment safety audits | Accessibility and safety affect participation differentially by city and income, but little effect on employment conditional on participating |

Gaps found, worth knowing: no rigorous causal Addis Ababa study, no Jakarta
employment study, no Indian instrumental-variable design using historic route
placement, no synthetic control applied to an Indian bus scheme. BBUS could be
first on the last two.

## D. Accessibility to jobs

| Source | Method | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Levinson et al., Access Across America: Transit | Travel time to jobs from every census block, time-decay weighted in 10-min steps to 60 min, aggregated to metro | The weighting approach, rather than a hard cutoff, is the part to copy |
| Peralta-Quiros, Kerzhner & Avner 2019, World Bank PRWP 8971 | Same method applied consistently across 11 African cities | This is the closest existing template for a comparative six-Indian-city benchmark |
| ITDP, People Near Transit | Share of residents within walking distance of adequate-frequency transit; already applied in Indian cities | Chennai reported at 61%. ITDP India is a plausible partner rather than a competitor |

Methodological caution: cumulative-opportunity and gravity-based accessibility
measures often correlate below 0.7. The choice of measure changes which wards
look well served. Report both, or justify one.

## E. Household transport expenditure burden

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Baker, Basu, Cropper, Lall & Takeuchi 2005, World Bank PRWP 3693; later Cropper & Bhattacharya, PRWP 4395 | Mumbai. Households under Rs 5,000/month spend about 16% of income on transport, about 10% on bus and rail fares alone. Poorest 27% receive only 19% of bus subsidy. The subsidy is regressive |
| NSSO/MoSPI Household Consumption Expenditure Survey 2022-23 | Conveyance is the largest non-food spending category. Get the unit-level microdata, do not cite news summaries |

The subsidy-incidence method in the Mumbai work is directly replicable and is
politically powerful: it shows who actually receives public money.

## F. Value of travel time savings

| Source | Note |
|---|---|
| World Bank meta-analysis of VTTS in low and middle income countries | Highest-value single document for an India benchmark. Obtain and read in full |
| ITF/OECD Roundtable 176, What is the Value of Saving Travel Time | Methodological framing, income elasticity of VTTS in LMICs |
| UK DfT TAG, underlying study Batley et al., Transportation | Recent revision raised commuting VTTS about 50%, cut non-work about 25% |
| US DOT BCA Guidance 2025 update | Official hourly benchmarks |
| Parmar, Saiyed & Dave, arXiv 2109.01045 | Cites bus-user VTTS about Rs 18.6/hour in Mumbai against Rs 132-179/hour for car users. This is a SECONDARY citation of Chepuri et al. 2020, which was not located. Verify before use |

The bus-versus-car VTTS gap matters enormously for the CBA. If bus users' time
is valued at a seventh of car users' time, then time-savings-led appraisal will
always favour roads. Say this explicitly in the report.

## H. Education access

| Source | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Muralidharan & Prakash 2017, AEJ: Applied | TRIPLE DIFFERENCE, Bihar cycle programme, boys and Jharkhand as comparisons | Girls' age-appropriate secondary enrolment up 32%; gender gap cut about 40%; effect driven by villages where school was further away, confirming a transport mechanism; more cost-effective than comparable cash transfers |

Bicycles, not buses, but this is the most rigorous transport-and-education study
found and the design adapts directly to a student bus pass.

## I. Safety and harassment

| Source | Method | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Borker 2021, World Bank PRWP 9731 | Student survey plus route mapping plus SafetiPin (26,500 audits) and Safecity (5,500 reports) to score routes, then a college choice model | Women choose a bottom-half college over a top-quintile one for a safer route; men in comparable choice sets pick top-third. Over 89% reported harassment while travelling |
| Garg 2024, ISID/ACEGD | Staggered Delhi Metro openings, DiD by police jurisdiction, distance bands | Reported harassment down about 29-32% after a station opens, within roughly 1 km. THE TWO FIGURES CONFLICT ACROSS SOURCES. Resolve against the primary paper |
| Sushmitha, Umadevi & Eashwar Anantha 2024, Indian J Community Medicine 49(7) | Cross-sectional survey, n=270 women, Chennai | 21.1% reported harassment; only 4 of those complained to police. The under-reporting gap is the methodological point |
| Bhatt, Menon & Khan 2015, EMBARQ India / WRI | Bhopal pilot | Dated, but useful precedent for instrument design |
| ITF/OECD Gender Analysis Toolkit for Transport Policies | Institutional framework | Adoptable measurement structure |

The under-reporting finding matters for BBUS: police records will understate
harassment by roughly an order of magnitude, so a survey instrument is not
optional if safety is to be measured.
